Meet Ryan Ellison, RPT

A musical family

I like to think my journey into piano technology started at my birth. I was born to two musical parents, both high-school orchestra directors and conductors, and talented musicians in their own right. Our home videos are full of me singing songs, or singing a duet with my dad.

I like to say I had "no choice" about becoming a musician. In my family, practicing music was a requirement from about the age of eight. I joined a children's choir and took piano lessons through elementary school.

When I was about twelve, I decided to follow in my father's footsteps and play the string bass. It started at a summer music camp, and I kept at it through junior high, high school and college. I played in orchestras, jazz bands and just about every kind of ensemble under the sun. If there was a group, chances are I was in it as the bassist.

After fifteen years of studying and performing music, I decided to go in a different direction.

Finding piano tuning

After college I landed a job at a music store in north Denver, where I was lucky enough to learn several instrument repair trades: woodwinds, brass, guitars, string instruments and electronics. Then the years after the 2008 financial crisis caught up with Main Street, and by 2010 the store was downsizing, ready or not.

That's when I took a leap of faith and chased a curiosity I'd had for a while: piano tuning. Pianos were one of the few instruments the store didn't service at all. We were constantly fielding calls for tuning and referring people to local piano technicians. While I was looking for technicians to send our customers to, I ran into the Piano Technicians Guild, and something clicked. I thought, "I bet I can do this."

I picked up the family piano from my grandparents' home, along with an amateur set of tuning tools my father owned (yes, that technically makes me a second-generation tuner). With the tools, a digital guitar tuner and a YouTube video, I set out to tune my family's Baldwin Hamilton console. Nope! I remember it so vividly: "This is SO difficult!" I had no idea what was happening when I put the tuning lever on the pin, and the piano ended up far worse than when I started. That's when I realized you can't learn to tune a piano without formal training.

Chicago

The Guild's website listed a few piano technology schools in the US. After one very important phone call to the director of the Chicago School for Piano Technology, I was convinced, and I enrolled in the one-year basic piano technology program, starting in August 2011.

At 26, I left my path as a musician and moved to Chicago. The school was on the second floor of a historic warehouse in Ukrainian Village. It was a rigorous four-semester certificate program, with over 1,100 hours of classwork and skills testing in aural tuning, regulation and repair, and it met or exceeded the Guild's own testing standards. After that year, I was ready to start tuning pianos out in the world.

Starting out in Denver

I came home to Denver with my tuning lever in my pocket and launched my first piano tuning business in October 2012. I was sure I'd get plenty of business from my old musician circles in Denver and northern Colorado. Wrong! I knew tons of guitarists from the rock scene, and horn players and drummers from the jazz scene, and I knew zero pianists. So I started the way every tuner does: pounding the pavement, putting up Craigslist ads and calling piano stores.

Then a local piano store owner gave me a chance. I'm sure he still wonders what he was thinking when I showed up with long hair to "tune" a piano for him, and I'm very grateful he did. His shop sold fine new European pianos and new Yamahas, and it had a full rebuilding shop where a number of large Steinway and Baldwin grands were completely remanufactured during my three years there. Slowly, but surely, I also built up a small private clientele of about 300 pianos in the Denver area by 2015.

West to Los Angeles, and Yamaha

The winds of change were blowing again, this time to the west. By late 2015 I had decided to move to the West Coast. After building my own small business over three hard-fought years, I didn't feel enthusiastic about doing it all over again, and the number of piano technicians and Guild luminaries in the LA area intimidated me. The competition looked severe, so I contacted piano stores looking for a job. After two job-hunting trips to LA, I still didn't have a solid piano job.

My last appointment on the last day of my last trip was with a Yamaha dealer in Van Nuys. He told me Yamaha was hiring and suggested I call the company about the job. That call was another one I'm so glad I made. In December 2015, I started my first day at Yamaha in Buena Park, California.

In my first years at Yamaha I was surrounded by longtime staff who carried a long oral history of the standards, the processes and the goals of piano technology in piano sales. In 2016 I took Yamaha's Performance Piano Service Seminar from a consultant, and the company's liaison manager at the time was a marvelous technician from Osaka who brought the special technique of projection piano tuning to the US.

Under his guidance I went through the very humbling process of starting over in my piano technical skills. Or rather, of un-learning many unproductive habits I'd picked up and replacing them with proven Yamaha method skills. I learned the Yamaha Piano Technical Academy style of instruction from the beginning: unison, octave, fifth, fourth, and how to work with a slow-beating fourths-and-fifths temperament. His pianos always sounded calm and powerful at the same time, and I had to rethink, and hear differently, aspects of piano sound I thought I already knew.

That's the road that brought me here. Today I put all of it to work for you: the musician's ear, the repair-shop curiosity, the Chicago training and ten years of Yamaha methods, one piano at a time.
